FEC Donor Watch · Baseline Report

Campaign-money concentration is shifting toward sector networks and late-cycle deployment

September 5, 2026. This first report establishes the comparison baseline and highlights material developments reported from August 27 through September 5. It tracks disclosed money, issue alignment, observed election outcomes and evidence of possible influence while explicitly separating correlation from causation.

Bottom line: Three patterns stand out. First, large pools of already-raised money are beginning to move into general-election races. Second, crypto, online betting and AI/data-center interests are accounting for an unusually large share of disclosed corporate political money. Third, some donor activity sits close to live regulatory decisions, which warrants monitoring but is not evidence of a quid pro quo.

Donor and network map

Elon Musk → America PAC

America PAC is a Musk-backed independent-expenditure-only committee. Its regular FEC report through June 30 shows $50.33M in receipts for the 2025–26 cycle, while September 24/48-hour-style disclosures reported by Reuters and CBS show a new burst of general-election spending that is not yet reflected in that regular summary.

Issue adjacency: Reuters notes several supported senators sit on committees overseeing federal spending or defense, areas relevant to companies controlled by Musk, including SpaceX. That overlap is relevant to an influence watch, but committee jurisdiction plus political support does not establish preferential treatment or a reciprocal arrangement.

Crypto companies → Fairshake / Protect Progress / Defend American Jobs

Fairshake’s FEC report through July 31 shows $137.41M in receipts, $88.66M in disbursements and $112.99M cash on hand. Its affiliates have also been active: Protect Progress reported $28.60M in receipts and $26.41M in disbursements through July 31; Defend American Jobs reported $38.27M in receipts and $33.84M in disbursements.

Issue interest: federal crypto-market structure, stablecoin rules and opposition to state or federal policies viewed as restrictive to digital-asset businesses.

AI / venture network → Leading the Future / Think Big / Build American AI

Leading the Future has been funded by technology and venture figures including Greg and Anna Brockman and Andreessen Horowitz; its affiliated Think Big PAC reported $20.52M in receipts and $19.90M in disbursements through July 31. The network’s issue focus is a national AI framework, resistance to a fragmented state regulatory regime, and support for AI infrastructure.

Counter-network: Anthropic has funded Public First Action, which supports stronger AI safeguards and state-level regulatory authority. AI money therefore does not represent a single policy position; competing industry factions are financing opposing regulatory strategies.

Online betting companies → Win for America

Public Citizen’s Q2 analysis reports that Win for America grew from $43M to $72M after receiving $14.5M from DraftKings, $7.5M from FanDuel, $5.5M from bet365 and additional industry money. The concentration indicates a coordinated sector effort rather than diffuse corporate giving.

Observed election outcomes — not a causal “success score”

For the current general election, it is too early to infer effectiveness because most newly targeted races will not be decided until November. Historical and primary results are mixed enough that a single success metric would overstate what the evidence can support.

  • AI groups: The Washington Post reported in March that 19 of 20 candidates receiving AI-industry support in early Texas and North Carolina primaries had won or advanced. Later contests showed more variation, including large, competing AI expenditures in Illinois and a costly Leading the Future intervention against New York candidate Alex Bores, who lost his primary.
  • Crypto groups: Fairshake entered 2026 after a strong 2024 election record, but Illinois produced clear setbacks. It spent more than $10M against Juliana Stratton, who won the Democratic Senate primary, and nearly $2.5M opposing La Shawn Ford, who also won. In another House primary, a candidate opposed by roughly $800,000 in Fairshake spending lost.
  • Interpretation: These are observed outcomes, not proof that outside spending caused wins or losses. Candidate quality, incumbency, endorsements, counter-spending, district partisanship, media coverage and local issues are major confounders.

Methodology and limitations

  • FEC committee summaries can lag newly filed activity by up to 48 hours. Regular committee summaries also may not include the newest 24- and 48-hour independent-expenditure reports.
  • Independent expenditures are spending made to support or oppose candidates without giving the money directly to the candidate; federal law prohibits coordination between a super PAC’s independent expenditures and the supported candidate.
  • “Corporate spending” totals cited from Public Citizen are an analysis of disclosed corporate contributions of $5,000 or more to super PACs and hybrid PACs, using FEC and OpenSecrets data. They exclude some nonprofit/dark-money activity and therefore should be treated as a disclosed minimum rather than a complete universe.
  • Announced fundraising totals, pledges and intended spending can differ from FEC-filed receipts and disbursements. This report privileges filed figures when available and labels announced amounts separately.
  • Issue alignment is coded from committee descriptions, donor business interests, public statements and reported targeting. It is not treated as evidence of corruption, contractual reward or quid pro quo.
