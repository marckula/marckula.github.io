FEC Donor Watch · September 15, 2026

A $77.3 million filing wave is dominated by opposition spending as newly formed super PACs obscure the donor picture

The clearest new signal is not a single donor check but the speed and structure of late-cycle outside spending. Independent-expenditure committees disclosed roughly $77.3 million across federal races from September 11–13, with nearly $68 million—about 88%—reported as opposition. SLF PAC and No Going Back PAC accounted for the largest Senate-scale bursts. Texas PAC also moved from ad reservations into large candidate-specific Schedule E activity. The donor picture is uneven: SLF has a substantial disclosed history plus an affiliated nonprofit funding channel, while No Going Back and Texas PAC are so new that full receipt disclosure is not yet available.

Executive summary: A review of the newest independent-expenditure filings found $77.3M reported from Sept. 11–13, of which about 88% opposed candidates. SLF PAC reported $25.0M on Sept. 12 across seven Senate races, all as opposition expenditures; No Going Back PAC reported about $21.1M on Sept. 13; House Majority PAC reported $4.7M across 13 House races Friday and Congressional Leadership Fund reported $7.7M across 15 House races Saturday. In Texas, Texas PAC reported $6.6M on Sept. 13—about $4.9M supporting Ken Paxton and $1.8M opposing James Talarico. Separately, media coverage of a later FEC filing describes more than $53.8M in Texas PAC pro-Paxton activity. Because Texas PAC is an independent-expenditure-only committee, that amount must not be described as a direct contribution to or receipt by Paxton's campaign; the FEC states that super PAC funds are not directly given to candidates. No credible evidence reviewed today ties these expenditures to a governmental appointment, contract, regulatory favor or quid pro quo.

Campaign and issue-interest developments

Opposition is the dominant late-cycle spending form

The Sept. 11–13 filings show a strong preference for negative independent expenditures: roughly $68M of $77.3M. In the biggest Senate race of the weekend, Ohio drew about $16M, led by SLF's roughly $8.4M and No Going Back's roughly $6.7M opposing Sherrod Brown. North Carolina drew about $7.8M, primarily opposition to Roy Cooper; Michigan drew about $6.9M, primarily opposition to Abdul El-Sayed; Iowa drew about $5.4M, with most of the largest lines opposing Josh Turek.

Texas PAC's scale is rising faster than processed databases can normalize it

The most defensible fresh transaction-level figure is the Sept. 13 Schedule E activity summarized from filings: $4.9M support for Paxton and $1.8M opposition to Talarico. Late Sept. 14 reporting of a newer filing puts the broader pro-Paxton figure above $53.8M, while AdImpact had already identified $51.3M in reserved broadcast advertising. Those figures should not simply be added together; they can describe overlapping commitments through different reporting systems and time windows.

The major donor trend is disclosure asymmetry

SLF has a mature donor history, but roughly 28% of its receipts through June came from One Nation, an affiliated nonprofit whose underlying donors are not publicly disclosed. No Going Back and Texas PAC present a different transparency problem: both were created shortly before major spending began, so their donor disclosures lag their expenditures. The result is a period when voters can see who is being targeted but not yet fully see who supplied the money.

Crypto policy remains a live outcome watch, not a completed outcome

The Fairshake/crypto-network baseline from the prior report remains relevant, but there is no final new legislative outcome to score this morning. The Senate is preparing for a Sept. 15 vote on major cryptocurrency market-structure legislation after negotiators added ethics and state-enforcement provisions. The timing is notable given large crypto-sector political spending, but it does not establish that political contributions caused the bill's text or trajectory.

Methodology and limitations

  • Freshness: today's update focuses on filings and reporting newly available after the Sept. 14 report, especially 24/48-hour independent-expenditure disclosures summarized Sept. 14.
  • Regular reports vs. 24/48-hour notices: FEC committee overview pages state that their displayed independent-expenditure totals are drawn from regular reports and do not include 24- and 48-hour IE reports. For late-cycle activity, recent notices can therefore be much larger than the summary-page figure.
  • Super PAC spending is not a candidate contribution: the FEC states that independent-expenditure-only committees may raise unlimited funds for independent political activity but do not make contributions to federal candidates. References to an outside group “giving” tens of millions to a campaign are avoided unless an actual lawful candidate contribution is documented.
  • Reservations vs. expenditures: media-reservation estimates, contracted placements, disbursements and Schedule E independent expenditures are related but not interchangeable. They are not added together when they may represent overlapping activity.
  • Donor attribution: a donor to a super PAC is not assumed to have funded a particular ad. Money routed through nonprofits with undisclosed underlying donors is labeled as opaque rather than assigned to an unknown individual or company.
  • Legislation: official Congress.gov and state-legislature sources are used for bill sponsorship, status and votes. A legislative record is presented as policy context unless there is independent evidence linking a donor's stated purpose to that specific policy.
  • Causation: electoral wins, legislative action, access or policy alignment are not treated as rewards for contributions without credible evidence connecting the two.
