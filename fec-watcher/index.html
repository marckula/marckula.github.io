FEC Donor Watch · September 19, 2026

AI and crypto-linked donor networks converge on Mike Rounds as South Dakota becomes a policy test case

The material new development is a newly documented concentration of technology-linked money around South Dakota Sen. Mike Rounds. WIRED reported on September 18 that Rounds has benefited this cycle from roughly $600,000 in outside spending, mostly from Defend American Jobs, plus about $215,000 in direct contributions from employees and PACs affiliated with AI labs and major technology companies. Separately, the AI-focused super PAC Leading the Future announced a $2 million general-election ad campaign supporting Rounds and three other Republican Senate candidates. The money coincides with Rounds's active work on AI, semiconductor and algorithmic-health legislation, while South Dakota itself has been legislating data-center electricity, water and local-control rules. The overlap is evidence of policy alignment and concentrated political spending; it is not evidence that contributions caused legislative action.

Executive summary: Leading the Future's own FEC reports show $75.79 million in receipts through June 30. Two $25 million receipts from A16Z Capital Management plus $12.5 million each from Greg and Anna Brockman total $75 million, about 99.0% of all receipts; partnership-attribution memo entries must not be counted again. Defend American Jobs reports $38.27 million in receipts through July 31, of which $37 million, about 96.7%, came as transfers from affiliated committees; an FEC Schedule A filing documents an $8 million Fairshake transfer on February 9. The highlighted network supports Rounds; no reviewed source documented a comparable new independent-expenditure campaign opposing his independent challenger Brian Bengs. No credible evidence reviewed for this update establishes a quid pro quo, government contract, appointment, regulatory favor or legislative reward tied to these contributions.

Campaign and issue-interest developments

AI policy is now a documented spending rationale

Leading the Future publicly framed its new investment around a national AI-policy framework, competitiveness, economic opportunity and protections for workers, families and communities. WIRED reports that congressional aides view Rounds as a significant industry supporter as Congress prepares for additional AI-regulation debates. This establishes issue alignment; it does not establish that any donor directed a specific bill.

AI and crypto networks overlap through committee transfers

Defend American Jobs is a super PAC with $37M in affiliated-committee transfers this cycle. A Feb. 9 FEC filing identifies Fairshake as the source of an $8M transfer. Fairshake is the large crypto-focused super PAC previously tracked by this page. The overlap shows that political-finance networks organized around emerging technology can share funding channels even when their stated issue programs are not identical.

South Dakota has a live data-center policy conflict

South Dakota's 2026 SB 135, the “Data Center Bill of Rights for Citizens,” requires separate electricity-service terms for large data centers so they reimburse costs fairly attributed to their demand, preserves local regulatory authority and requires water-use review/reporting. Rounds is a federal senator and did not vote on this state bill. WIRED reports that he ultimately endorsed adding restrictions on data centers.

The challenger is using campaign-finance policy as an issue

Independent candidate Brian Bengs has no current legislative voting record. Dakota News Now reported Sept. 4 that Bengs pledged to reject AI/data-center money, while also saying he is not anti-data-center and views AI as important to U.S. competitiveness and national security. No reviewed evidence shows the highlighted super-PAC network funding Bengs.

Observed electoral and policy outcomes

No electoral outcome yet: no election result is recorded for the South Dakota Senate race. This report does not infer electoral effectiveness from spending.

Federal policy outcome: Rounds's S.1473 passed the Senate on May 20, 2026 and was sent to the House. His S.2528 and S.1399 remain at the introduced/referred stage in the official records reviewed. This mixed record is important: documented industry-aligned giving coincides with both a bill that advanced and bills that have not advanced, which weighs against treating contributions as sufficient explanations for legislative outcomes.

State policy outcome: South Dakota's SB 135 moved through the state legislature with requirements aimed at preventing data-center electricity costs from being shifted to other customers, preserving local authority and protecting water supplies. Rounds is a federal senator and had no vote on SB 135. The state action demonstrates that AI/data-center policy is a live local issue independent of federal campaign spending.

Influence, access and potential reward evidence

Policy alignment is documented: Leading the Future says it is investing in candidates it believes will shape a national AI framework; Rounds sponsors legislation involving AI regulatory sandboxes, algorithmic health services and semiconductor export controls. That is evidence of alignment between a donor network's stated interests and a candidate's record, not evidence of an exchange.

Network/access overlap: WIRED reports that Rounds's senior aide and former chief of staff Robert Skjonsberg is registered to lobby for Meta on matters before the South Dakota legislature. WIRED also reports that Rounds's office told Politico its general counsel consulted the Senate Ethics Committee and was told there was no conflict of interest. This is a documented professional-network overlap; the reviewed evidence does not establish that it produced a legislative favor or regulatory outcome.

No documented reward: this review found no credible public evidence that the highlighted contributions or independent expenditures led to an appointment, government contract, regulatory exemption, special-access arrangement, or quid pro quo. South Dakota's enacted/passed data-center restrictions and Rounds's support for restrictions also complicate any simple claim that technology-sector money produced uniformly industry-favorable policy.

Methodology and limitations

  • Update threshold: the page changes only when new evidence materially changes spending scale, donor concentration, committee structure, candidate targeting, policy outcomes, donor geography or credible influence/access analysis. The newly documented South Dakota technology-money concentration met that threshold.
  • Reporting lag: the FEC's September Monthly reports are due Sept. 20 and cover activity through Aug. 31. Leading the Future's processed quarterly summary currently ends June 30; Defend American Jobs and Fairshake processed monthly summaries currently end July 31. Recent independent expenditures can therefore be visible before the newest donor receipts.
  • Partnership attribution: A16Z Capital Management's partnership contributions generate attribution memo entries for partners. The $50M partnership receipts and the partner attribution memos are not added together. The concentration calculation uses $50M A16Z + $12.5M Greg Brockman + $12.5M Anna Brockman = $75M against $75.788M total committee receipts.
  • Independent expenditures are not candidate contributions: Defend American Jobs and Leading the Future spending is legally separate from Rounds for Senate. It is not money received or controlled by Rounds's campaign.
  • Issue-network labels: “AI-linked,” “crypto-linked” and “technology-linked” describe publicly documented donors, committee affiliations, stated purposes and reporting; they do not imply that every donor shares an identical policy agenda.
  • Map standard: geographic origin is based on FEC-reported donor/attribution addresses. A PAC's registration or mailing address is administrative and is not treated as the geographic origin of its money. Missing or “best efforts” addresses are not guessed.
  • Non-legislators: Brian Bengs is explicitly identified as having no current legislative voting record; only verifiable stated positions are included.
  • No causal inference: timing, access, contributions, independent spending, sponsorship and votes are not treated as proof that one caused another without credible evidence.
